Analysis and Quantification of SRAM Cell Stability Using Static Noise Margin Metrics

A simulation-based investigation of 6T SRAM read stability using LTspice XVII. The Static Noise Margin is extracted through the butterfly curve method and characterized as a function of transistor sizing, supply voltage, and operating temperature in 180 nm CMOS technology.

180 nm TSMC CMOS LTspice XVII BSIM3v3 Level-3 DC Analysis Parametric Study Academic Year 2024–25
01 — Background and Motivation
A

What is SRAM and why does it matter?

Static Random Access Memory (SRAM) is the dominant memory technology used in processor caches, register files, and on-chip memory in system-on-chip (SoC) designs. Unlike DRAM, SRAM does not need to be refreshed periodically because it uses a bistable latch to hold data. This makes it significantly faster, but also larger and more power-hungry per bit. In modern processors, more than 70% of the chip area is typically occupied by SRAM arrays, which makes the design of each individual cell critically important for overall chip performance and reliability.

As CMOS technology scales into the nanometre regime, SRAM stability becomes a growing concern. Smaller transistors suffer from increased threshold voltage variability, leakage current, and reduced noise immunity. A cell that is not stable enough will flip its stored bit even in the absence of a deliberate write operation, which causes a data error. Our project focuses on quantifying and optimising this stability.

B

What is the Static Noise Margin?

The Static Noise Margin (SNM) is a single number, measured in millivolts, that tells us how much DC noise voltage the storage nodes of an SRAM cell can tolerate simultaneously before the cell loses its stored value. It was formally defined by Seevinck, List, and Lohstroh in their 1987 IEEE JSSC paper, which remains the foundational reference in this field.

Intuition: Imagine two people pushing against each other in a tug of war. As long as both are equally matched, the rope stays in place. SNM is a measure of how hard an external force needs to push before one person falls over. A higher SNM means the cell is more robust against noise, process variation, and temperature fluctuations.

There are two distinct SNM values we measure for a 6T SRAM cell. The Hold SNM is measured when the word line is low (the cell is in standby). The Read SNM is measured when the word line is asserted and the bit lines are precharged, which is the critical and more vulnerable condition because the access transistors partially disturb the storage nodes during a read operation.

C

Why is read stability the bottleneck?

During a read operation, the word line is raised to VDD and both bit lines are precharged. When the cell is storing a logic 0 at node Q, the access transistor M5 connects Q to BL. A voltage divider forms between M5 and M3: M5 tries to pull Q up toward VDD, while M3 tries to hold it at ground. If M5 is too strong relative to M3, the voltage at Q rises high enough to trigger the second inverter and accidentally flip the cell. This phenomenon is called a read-disturb error.

To prevent this, the pull-down transistor (M3) must be made stronger than the access transistor (M5). The ratio of their drive strengths is called the cell ratio (CR). A higher cell ratio means a more stable read operation, but it also means a larger cell area. Our parametric study maps exactly how the Read SNM varies with the cell ratio so that an optimal point can be identified.

02 — 6T SRAM Cell Architecture

The six-transistor (6T) SRAM cell consists of two cross-coupled CMOS inverters forming a bistable latch, plus two NMOS access transistors controlled by the word line. Each component has a specific electrical role that directly influences stability.

6T SRAM cell — schematic topology

Transistor roles — detailed explanation

Pull-up devices — M1, M2 (PMOS)

M1 and M2 are PMOS transistors connected between VDD and the storage nodes Q and QB respectively. Their gates are driven by the complementary storage node. When QB is low (0 V), M1 turns on and actively pulls node Q to VDD, reinforcing the stored logic 1. A wider PMOS pull-up (larger W/L) increases the regenerative drive strength of the latch but also increases cell area and leakage current.

Pull-down devices — M3, M4 (NMOS)

M3 and M4 are NMOS transistors connected between the storage nodes and ground. Their gates are driven by the opposite storage node. When Q is high, M4 turns on and actively holds QB at ground, reinforcing the stored logic 0. During a read operation, M3 must be strong enough to resist the pull-up action of the access transistor M5. This is why the pull-down transistors are typically wider than the access transistors — governed by the cell ratio.

Access devices — M5, M6 (NMOS)

M5 and M6 are the pass-gate transistors controlled by the word line (WL). When WL is asserted to VDD, both transistors turn on, connecting Q to BL and QB to BLB. During a read, the precharged bit lines create a current path through the access transistor and the pull-down transistor. During a write, the bit line voltage must overpower the pull-up transistor through the access transistor, which is why the pull-up ratio is kept low (access stronger than pull-up).

Cross-coupling — the bistable latch

The output of the first inverter (node Q) feeds the gate of the second inverter (M2, M4), and the output of the second inverter (node QB) feeds the gate of the first inverter (M1, M3). This positive feedback creates two stable states: Q=1, QB=0 and Q=0, QB=1. Any perturbation that is smaller than the SNM is self-corrected by the regenerative action of the latch. A perturbation larger than the SNM causes the cell to flip.

03 — Static Noise Margin — Theory
1

Voltage Transfer Characteristic (VTC)

The Voltage Transfer Characteristic of an inverter is a plot of its output voltage against its input voltage. For a CMOS inverter operating on VDD = 1.8 V, the VTC is an S-shaped curve. When the input is low (near 0 V), the PMOS pull-up is fully on and the output is high (near VDD). As the input rises through the switching threshold, the output falls sharply. When the input is near VDD, the NMOS pull-down is fully on and the output is near ground. The steepness of the transition region and the exact switching threshold are determined by the W/L ratios of the PMOS and NMOS transistors.

2

The Butterfly Curve

The butterfly curve is constructed by superimposing the VTC of the first cross-coupled inverter and the inverse VTC of the second inverter on the same set of axes. The horizontal axis represents the voltage at node QB and the vertical axis represents the voltage at node Q. The two curves intersect at three points: two stable operating points at the extremes (Q=1, QB=0 and Q=0, QB=1) and one unstable equilibrium at the centre. The resulting shape resembles the wings of a butterfly, which gives the method its name.

Each wing (or lobe) of the butterfly contains a region where the cell would stably settle. The size of each lobe directly indicates how much noise the cell can tolerate. A larger, more open lobe means more noise immunity.

3

SNM Extraction — Inscribed Square Method

The SNM is defined as the side length of the largest square that can be inscribed within each lobe of the butterfly curve, with the square oriented at 45 degrees to the axes. The square is constrained to fit entirely within the eye of the butterfly. A larger square means a higher SNM and therefore a more stable cell.

Mathematically, the tangency condition for the inscribed square requires that both VTC curves have a slope of −1 at the points where the corners of the square touch the curves. This yields a system of simultaneous equations that can be solved numerically once the simulation data is available.

SNM = max { min [ Vin − Vout(VTC1), Vout − Vin(VTC2) ] }

In practice, the SNM is read directly from the LTspice waveform viewer by identifying the largest square that fits within the butterfly lobe. The side length of this square, measured in millivolts, is the SNM. The smaller of the two lobe SNMs is reported as the overall cell SNM.

Butterfly curve — annotated illustration

Factors that affect the SNM

Supply voltage VDD

The SNM scales approximately linearly with VDD at high supply voltages. As VDD is reduced below 1.2 V, the transistors begin to approach their threshold voltages and the VTC transition becomes less steep. This reduces the size of the butterfly lobe sharply, causing the SNM to degrade rapidly. Sub-threshold operation (VDD below threshold voltage) reduces the SNM to near zero, making the cell unreliable without alternative cell topologies.

Cell ratio (transistor sizing)

Increasing the cell ratio (making M3 wider relative to M5) directly improves the Read SNM. A wider pull-down transistor clamps the voltage rise at node Q more effectively during a read, keeping the butterfly lobe open. However, a higher cell ratio also increases the minimum cell area since M3 occupies more silicon. The optimal cell ratio for 180 nm technology is typically in the range of 1.5 to 2.0, balancing stability against area efficiency.

Temperature

At higher temperatures, the threshold voltage of MOSFETs decreases (approximately −2 mV per Kelvin for bulk CMOS at 180 nm) and carrier mobility degrades due to increased phonon scattering. The net effect on SNM is a modest degradation at elevated temperatures because the switching threshold of the inverters shifts and the drive current of the pull-down transistors reduces. At low temperatures, the SNM typically improves slightly due to higher carrier mobility.

Process variation

At nanometre scales, statistical fluctuations in the number and position of dopant atoms in the transistor channel cause cell-to-cell variation in threshold voltage. This means different cells in the same SRAM array have slightly different SNMs. The tail of the SNM distribution determines the array-level failure rate. In our simulation we model the nominal process corner, but the parametric study provides insight into how sensitive the SNM is to device parameter changes.

04 — Methodology
Simulation tool

LTspice XVII (Analog Devices). Free, widely validated SPICE simulator. Supports DC sweep, parametric step, and Monte Carlo analyses.

MOSFET model

BSIM3v3 Level-3 models parameterised for TSMC 180 nm process. Vth,N ≈ 0.35 V, Vth,P ≈ −0.37 V, tox = 4.1 nm.

01

Netlist construction and model inclusion

The 6T SRAM cell netlist is written in SPICE syntax and loaded into LTspice. The six transistors are instantiated with their drain, gate, source, and bulk connections. Parameters for W and L are declared using the .param directive so they can be swept without rewriting the netlist. The MOSFET model file (180nm_models.lib) is included using the .inc directive.

A voltage source Vin is connected to the QB node (the input of inverter 1). This is the sweep variable. A separate voltage source sets up the read condition: WL = VDD, BL = VDD, BLB = VDD.

02

DC sweep simulation

The .dc Vin 0 {VDD} 5m command sweeps Vin from 0 to 1.8 V in 5 mV steps. At each step, the simulator solves for the DC operating point of the entire circuit. The voltages at nodes Q and QB are recorded at every step. This produces two data columns that each trace one wing of the butterfly curve.

The sweep takes less than one second in LTspice. The resolution of 5 mV is sufficient to accurately capture the steep transition region of the VTC and to extract the SNM without significant quantisation error.

03

Butterfly curve construction in LTspice waveform viewer

After the simulation completes, the butterfly curve is constructed in the LTspice waveform viewer using the following steps:

Step A: Right-click the waveform area and select “Add Trace.” Enter V(Q) as the Y expression. Right-click the horizontal axis and select “Expression” and enter V(QB_in). This plots the VTC of inverter 1: Q as a function of the sweep input.

Step B: Add a second trace. Enter V(QB_in) as the Y expression and V(QB) as the X expression. This plots the VTC of inverter 2 with axes interchanged, giving the inverse characteristic.

Step C: The two S-shaped curves together form the butterfly. Visually locate the largest square that fits inside each lobe. The side length of this square in volts is the SNM. Alternatively, export the data using File → Export Data and process numerically to find the exact inscribed square.

04

Parametric study

Three independent parametric sweeps are conducted. In each sweep, one parameter is varied while the others are held at their baseline values. For each parameter value, the full DC sweep is re-executed, the butterfly curve is regenerated, and the SNM is extracted and recorded.

Design trade-off summary for 180 nm

A cell ratio of 1.33 (our baseline) is on the lower end of typical designs and provides a Read SNM of approximately 280–320 mV. Increasing the cell ratio to 2.0 by widening M3 and M4 to 540 nm improves the Read SNM to approximately 380–420 mV, which provides a comfortable margin above the 200 mV minimum. However, this increases the cell footprint by roughly 15–20%, which is significant in a dense memory array where millions of cells are replicated. Our study will identify the minimum cell ratio that achieves a Read SNM above 250 mV across the full temperature range of −40°C to 100°C.

The pull-up ratio of 0.5 means the pull-up transistors are twice as wide as the access transistors. This provides adequate hold stability but means the access transistors must be even more precisely sized so that a write operation can overpower the pull-up. If the access transistors are made too narrow (to improve read SNM), the write margin degrades and the cell becomes write-fail prone. This fundamental tension is the core design challenge of 6T SRAM and is the reason our parametric study covers both pull-down and access transistor widths.
